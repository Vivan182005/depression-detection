# Honest depression detection tops out near 0.80 AUC

The short answer is that our pipeline is already at the top of what honestly evaluated speech and text models achieve on DAIC-WOZ and E-DAIC. Published numbers above about 0.85 F1 or 90% accuracy almost all rest on one of three flaws. **The interviewer-prompt shortcut** lets text models score 0.84-0.96 F1 because Ellie's follow-up questions are only asked when the participant reports mental-health history ([Burdisso et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14463v1)). **Speaker overlap** between train and test lifts the same wav2vec2 model from 58.7% to 97.7% accuracy ([arXiv 2604.14354](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14354v1)). **Tuning and reporting on the 35-person dev set** makes one audio model look like 0.81 F1 on the dev set and 0.576 F1 / 0.599 AUC under subject-level cross-validation ([Li et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08593)). Once those flaws are removed, speaker-independent results sit at roughly 0.55-0.74 macro-F1. The best protocol-matched references are an E-DAIC leave-one-subject-out text+LLM model at AUROC 0.768 / macro-F1 0.723 and an LLM-only scorer at AUROC 0.825 ([Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977)). Against that, our AUC 0.764 and 74.3% accuracy (majority baseline 68.8%) on 272 speakers under repeated nested CV are competitive and, by sample size and protocol, more trustworthy than most of the literature. The teacher's accuracy target has one structural problem. Because 69% of our participants are non-depressed, accuracy moves slowly with AUC: under a binormal model that reproduces our 74.3% exactly, raising AUC to 0.80 yields only about 76% accuracy, and 0.85 yields about 80%. The one literature-backed lever with a realistic chance of a measurable gain is a local LLM that scores the eight PHQ-8 items from the transcript, added as a stacker feature. Turn-level modelling, an extra WavLM backbone, ordinal item heads and visual features are cheaper second-tier bets, each worth roughly 0 to +0.02 AUC.

A note on sourcing. Every number below comes from the research notes and carries a link. Numbers marked **[secondary]** were read from another paper's comparison table, not from the original. Numbers marked **[unverified]** come from abstracts or search snippets only, and should not be quoted to examiners without checking the PDF. Calculations marked **(computed here)** are ours, not from a source.

The pattern in the table is consistent. **Reported performance falls as protocols tighten.** Burdisso et al. found that the prompt-using papers "exhibit an overall superior performance", and that a model reading only Ellie's turns (0.88 F1) beats one reading only the participant (0.85) on the dev set. The signal sits in the second half of the interview, where Ellie asks "Have you been diagnosed with depression?" and branches into therapy follow-ups ([Burdisso 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14463v1)). Watawana et al. traced interviewer-only models to a handful of prompts such as "Do you still go to therapy?" and "How do you cope with that?" ([Watawana 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24651v2)). The paper-to-prompt mapping in Burdisso's table was reconstructed from a flattened HTML table and should be confirmed against the PDF before it is cited by name.

Speaker overlap is the second leak and the largest single effect in the literature. The controlled 2026 experiment held the 38-speaker test set fixed and only changed whether those speakers also appeared in training. A frozen wav2vec2 linear probe went from F1 0.528 to 0.765; a fine-tuned one went from 0.562 to 0.976, while a speaker-ID probe reached 91-96%. In the authors' framing, papers above 90% accuracy on DAIC-WOZ "may be inflated by leakage", and adversarial speaker removal did not close the gap ([arXiv 2604.14354](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14354v1)). Our notes attribute this preprint to "Yeh et al." in one place and "Sun et al." in another, so cite it by arXiv number until the authorship is confirmed.

The third leak is the small official evaluation set. DAIC-WOZ's dev set has 35 people (23 control, 12 depressed). Test labels were withheld for the AVEC challenge, so most papers report on dev, often after tuning on it ([Burdisso 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14463v1)). On that set, always predicting "not depressed" scores 65.7% accuracy, and each person is worth 2.9 accuracy points (computed here). The dev-to-test drop is large where it has been measured: participant-only GCN fell from 0.85 to 0.59 macro-F1 ([Watawana 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24651v2)), and a BERT summarisation approach fell from 0.81 to 0.67 ([arXiv 2409.08483](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.08483)). Even the larger official E-DAIC test set (56) does not stabilise rankings. Across 96 configurations, the best-by-CV model ranked 20th on the test set, the test winner ranked 41st by CV, and the "winner" was rank 1 in only 32.3% of subject bootstraps ([Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977)).

## Trustworthy results cluster between 0.55 and 0.74 F1

When the three leaks are removed, the honest numbers form a narrow band. Audio-only speaker-independent results run from macro-F1 0.55 (HuBERT on the DAIC-WOZ dev set) to 0.67 (HuBERT-large-superb-ER on the E-DAIC test) at the speaker level ([Gomez-Zaragoza 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/gomezzaragoza25_interspeech.pdf)). They reach 0.74 at the segment level with a fine-tuned weighted-layer wav2vec2 ([arXiv 2604.14354](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14354v1)). The only audio AUC under subject-level CV is 0.599 ([Li et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08593)). Participant-only text on the official test sets runs 0.40-0.68 macro-F1 ([Watawana 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24651v2)), and sentence-embedding text under E-DAIC leave-one-subject-out CV reaches AUROC 0.647 ([Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977)). Systematic reviews agree with this picture. A JMIR meta-analysis of 105 speech studies found that 47.6% had high risk of bias in at least one domain, and that pooled *lowest-reported* accuracy was 0.66 against 0.81 for *highest-reported* ([Maran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12590051/)). In clinical-speech ML more broadly, reported accuracy *falls* as sample size grows, which points to publication bias and overfitting ([Berisha et al. 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/berisha22_interspeech.html)). A DAIC-WOZ reproducibility review found that only 5 of 66 assessed papers met minimal reproducibility standards. It reported that without subject leakage, one model "consistently performed worse than a simple mean predictor" ([Danylenko & Unold 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/1/422), abstract and snippets only [unverified]).

Two results near the top of the honest band need a caveat each. Gomez-Zaragoza's E-DAIC audio+text F1 of 0.840 uses a sound protocol, but on a 56-person test set. Their own speech-only bootstrap standard deviation is about 0.06, so the true value is uncertain by roughly ±0.1 ([Gomez-Zaragoza 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/gomezzaragoza25_interspeech.pdf)). The LLM-only AUROC of 0.825 is the strongest honest single number. However, the LLM read the full dialogue including Ellie's turns, it is a 120-billion-parameter model, and the authors cannot rule out DAIC transcripts in its pretraining data ([Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977)). It is a real signal, but an upper bound for a participant-only, CPU-sized replication.

Accuracy itself is a weak yardstick here. Teferra et al.'s GPT-4o reached 75.9% accuracy but an MCC of only 0.25 ([Teferra 2025](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pdig.0000943)), which shows how a prevalence-driven majority baseline inflates accuracy. That is why the field's recommended protocol asks for speaker-disjoint splits, participant-only inputs, out-of-fold estimates with bootstrap CIs, AUC/MCC/balanced accuracy beside a majority baseline, no tuning on the reported set, and gender-stratified reporting ([Watawana 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24651v2); [Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977); [Maran 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12590051/); [Bailey & Plumbley 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15120)). Our project already meets every item except macro-F1/MCC reporting and external validation on another corpus.

## Our pipeline trades headline numbers for defensibility

Our project differs from the typical paper on almost every axis that the audits flag. The table below summarises the differences.

| Dimension | Typical DAIC-WOZ / E-DAIC paper | Our project | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evaluation set | 35 dev or 47/56 test, single split | 272 speakers, 5 folds x 5 seeds nested CV, plus a one-shot sealed 33 | AUC CI about ±0.065 (computed here, Hanley-McNeil) vs ±0.1 or more on 35-56 people |
| Speaker handling | often segment-level or unstated | speaker-disjoint folds | Removes the largest documented leak |
| Interviewer content | manual transcripts, often with Ellie | Silero VAD on participant audio; Whisper-tiny on participant audio | Removes the prompt shortcut; Ellie bleed measured at 3 of 61 recordings |
| Transcripts | manual (DAIC) or provided ASR (E-DAIC) | Whisper-tiny for all | Consistent across train and test, but the noisiest ASR in use |
| Aggregation | turn or utterance sequences (GRU, Transformer) | mean-pooled whole-session embeddings | Loses question/turn structure; also less overfitting at N=272 |
| Target | binary classifier, or regression scored by CCC | ridge on PHQ-8, CDF calibration, logistic stacker | Uses the full score range; simple, low-variance fusion |
| Modalities | often A+T+V | A+T only | No visual signal (literature says it is weak) |
| Corpora | one corpus | pooled DAIC-WOZ + E-DAIC Avatar | Bigger N; adds a possible corpus/prevalence confound |
| Reporting | F1 (often best seed) | AUC, accuracy, balanced accuracy, majority baseline, subgroup AUCs, bootstrap CIs, pre-registration | Meets audit recommendations |

The closest published relative is Gomez-Zaragoza et al. 2025. It uses the same HuBERT-large-superb-ER audio encoder and the same j-hartmann emotion-RoBERTa-large text encoder, but per-turn embeddings feed a GRU, and results are reported on official test splits ([Gomez-Zaragoza 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/gomezzaragoza25_interspeech.pdf)). Their speech-only results (0.55-0.67 F1) and ours (voice-only AUC about 0.76) are in different metrics. Both show the same thing: emotion-tuned frozen encoders give most of what audio can offer at this data size. They found female speakers scored 0.05-0.1 F1 lower. We do not see that gap: our female AUC (0.77) is slightly above our male AUC (0.75). That is worth reporting as a point of difference, with the caveat that subgroup CIs at our N are wide.

Our design also has blind spots. A spot check of one WoZ transcript (participant 380) confirmed that it contains only participant speech, with no Ellie questions. It does contain participant disclosures and direct address ("Put me on the spot here, Ellie"). Participants' answers to the conditional history questions are a legitimate but proxy-like source of label information that no paper has quantified ([Burdisso 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.14463v1)). Pooling two corpora carries the risk documented in a 2026 study of pooled depression corpora. There, corpus identity was recoverable at 97.5-99.8% accuracy, and a corpus-plus-prevalence oracle reached AUC 0.697 with no speech signal at all ([arXiv 2610.31247](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31247)). Our null results for domain mean-centring and per-domain thresholds suggest this is not driving our AUC, and the Avatar subgroup AUC (0.79) is higher than WoZ (0.75), not lower. Even so, the corpus-only AUC is a cheap number that examiners will ask for. Whisper-tiny is the weakest ASR used anywhere in this literature. Groups re-transcribe even E-DAIC with Whisper large-v3 because the provided transcripts are poor ([Lee et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12885269/)). Our notes found no study that measures how much ASR errors cost in depression detection ([Lopez-Otero 2017](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/lopezotero17b_interspeech.html) reports only a "slight" drop).

The project's null results line up with the literature. Augmentation, few-shot, chain-of-thought and synthetic data show no consistent honest gain in the published work either. Gemini-2.0-Flash fell from 0.84 zero-shot to 0.75 one-shot ([Merzougui 2025](https://maurelf.users.greyc.fr/docs/conferences/AIME_2025.pdf)), and synthetic-session papers report only on official splits ([PsyGAT 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24126)). Wu et al.'s sub-dialogue augmentation was essential, but only for end-to-end sequence models, not for frozen embeddings with ridge ([Wu 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.12263)).

The ranking puts the LLM scorer first for a specific reason. It is the only technique that adds information our encoders cannot represent: explicit symptom reasoning over content. In E-DAIC, text scores shift by +0.422 on symptom-dense versus symptom-light slices while audio shifts by -0.004 ([Ishikawa & Duke 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23977)). That suggests a content-aware text feature is complementary to our largely content-blind voice branch. The strongest LLM-scored correlates of PHQ-8 are depressed mood, negative sentiment and sleep disturbance ([Lee et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12885269/)), and all three are things participants talk about in DAIC interviews. Because our text branch already reaches AUC 0.74, well above the 0.647 embedding baseline the LLM beat, expect the gain to shrink well below the published +0.18.

The protocol for every experiment matters as much as the choice. Each change should be pre-registered as one comparison, not a search. It should be evaluated with a paired test on the same folds (paired bootstrap or DeLong), because the unpaired AUC CI of about ±0.065 would hide any of these gains (computed here). The sealed 33-person test has been used once. Re-using it to pick a winner would turn it into a second dev set; with 11 depressed people, each person there moves accuracy by 3 points.

## The realistic ceiling is about 0.80 AUC and 77% accuracy

Accuracy on this dataset is mostly set by prevalence, and that changes what "raising accuracy" can mean. Model the stacker's score as two equal-variance normal distributions with 68.8% negatives. Our AUC of 0.764 then implies a maximum accuracy of 74.3%, which is exactly our observed figure (computed here). On the same model, AUC 0.78 gives about 75.2% accuracy, AUC 0.80 about 76.4%, AUC 0.82 about 77.7% and AUC 0.85 about 79.8% (computed here). Every +0.02 AUC therefore buys roughly one accuracy point. Balanced accuracy and sensitivity move faster and tell the teacher more about whether depressed people are being caught.

Combining the literature with our own numbers, a realistic honest ceiling for this protocol, these 272 people and CPU-only methods is **AUC about 0.78-0.82, accuracy about 75-78% and balanced accuracy about 69-72%**. The lower end is what the second-tier experiments would plausibly reach stacked together. The upper end assumes the LLM item scorer carries over a meaningful fraction of its E-DAIC advantage. Nothing in the honest literature supports above 80% accuracy or 0.85 AUC on speaker-disjoint, participant-only DAIC-WOZ/E-DAIC. A result in that range would more likely mean a new leak than a breakthrough. The uncertainties are real. No paper reports AUC under our exact pooled protocol. The best LLM evidence comes from one preprint on one corpus. And our sealed-test estimate on 33 people is too small to confirm a 1-2 point change either way.

## Conclusion

The literature review changes how the project's numbers should be read. Our 74.3% accuracy looks modest beside published 90%+ figures, but those figures are artefacts of the interviewer shortcut, speaker overlap and 35-person dev sets. Measured against papers with honest protocols, our AUC 0.764 sits at the upper end of what has been achieved, on a larger and better-controlled sample than nearly all of them. The gap between our result and the honest ceiling is about 0.04-0.06 AUC, or 2-4 accuracy points. That is small enough that most of it can only be shown with paired statistics, not eyeballed.

For the teacher, the practical implication is that the next accuracy point is more likely to come from adding a new kind of information than from tuning the existing encoders. The candidate is LLM symptom scoring of what participants say, and the project's string of null results for augmentation and few-shot already shows that tuning has stalled. For the student, the strongest contribution may be to publish what the field lacks: a pre-registered, speaker-disjoint, pooled-corpus benchmark with AUC and corpus-only baselines. It would be the first such reference point the audits say is missing.
